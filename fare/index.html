01 — Logline

A sleepless rideshare driver picks up someone wearing a face buried in his past — pulling him toward a string of missing riders as his identity fractures.

02 — Synopsis

Synopsis

He makes his living being cancelled. A profile picture disturbing enough that nobody lets him arrive — the ride dies, the fee clears, and he never has to speak to a soul. It works every night. Until the night a fare won't cancel.

The passenger in his back seat knows things a stranger shouldn't. Riders have been vanishing along these roads — their posters fading on every light pole — and the route keeps pulling him past the last streets he recognizes, toward a tunnel that isn't on any map. He can still turn around. He keeps telling himself he can still turn around.

Somewhere past the tunnel, something is wearing his face better than he does.

03 — Director's vision

Director's Vision

FARE explores one of humanity's oldest fears through a modern lens: the loss of identity. In a world increasingly defined by profiles, photographs, ratings, and perception, the line between who we are and the versions of ourselves we project has never been thinner. The film asks what happens when that fabricated identity begins to take on a life of its own.

Through surreal horror, liminal spaces, and psychological dread, FARE explores the terror of watching your identity fracture until the person staring back at you is no longer someone you recognize. The horror relies on atmosphere, practical effects, distorted human imagery, and escalating dread rather than traditional jump scares.

04 — Visual style

Three Worlds

The film progresses through three distinct visual worlds, each with its own light, palette, and logic — a slow descent from the familiar into the impossible.

Home

Warm yellow · tungsten

Familiar, intimate, but quietly deteriorating. Lamplight, clutter, the residue of a life on pause.

The Road

Cold blues & greens · sodium

Rain-soaked highways, empty intersections, and growing isolation. The city thins out until only the route remains.

The Train

Deep crimson · impossible architecture

Endless repetition and dreamlike logic where reality no longer exists. Liminal corridors, flickering light, and the faces that appear in it.

Lookbook

Framing · dread · the watcher

Oppressive close-ups against expansive, liminal wides — intimate framing that traps the viewer with the protagonist, and empty space that invites the eye to search every corner of the frame.

Reference imagery. Frames drawn from contemporary horror cinema and found photography, curated for tone — not production footage.

05 — Locations

Where It Shoots

The Tunnel

The threshold of the film — the point where the road ends and the impossible begins. Scouted north of Los Angeles; shot at the mouth at night with practical sodium fixtures.

Location scout photograph — Srijan Acharya.

The House

Ellis's world before the fare: warm, lived-in, and quietly wrong. Practical interiors give the Home world its tungsten palette and its clutter — the coffee table where the film's first reveal hides in plain sight.

Location scout photographs — Srijan Acharya.

The Train & Stage

The film's third world shoots on a practical train-car set with full lighting control, with stage work at CinePacks Studios for the sequences where the architecture stops obeying the rules.
